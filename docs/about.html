Context of the Research

The Digital Health Paradox

Digital health technologies — health apps, wearable trackers, and online health services — have become a routine part of life for millions of people in the UK. Policymakers have positioned them as cost-effective tools for improving population health at scale (Rich, 2019; Mariano, 2020; Steinhubl, 2013), and the NHS Ten Year Plan has made the shift to digital healthcare a core strategic priority (Department of Health and Social Care, 2025).

Yet the benefits of digital health are not equally shared. People from lower socioeconomic backgrounds, older adults, and other disadvantaged groups are consistently less likely to benefit from digital health interventions — even when access to technology is not the issue (Western, 2021; Zhang, 2023; Birch, 2022; Badr, 2024). This is sometimes called the digital health paradox: technologies designed to improve health for everyone may, in practice, widen existing health inequalities.

Why Do Inequalities Persist?

Researchers have identified three levels at which digital divides operate (van Dijk, 2020):

  • Level 1 — Material access: who has devices and internet connectivity
  • Level 2 — Skills and usage: who can use technology effectively
  • Level 3 — Outcomes: who actually benefits, even after access and skills barriers are overcome

Most existing research and policy has focused on Levels 1 and 2. But outcome inequalities remain even when people have both access and basic skills. One systematic review and meta-analysis found that individuals in the lowest socioeconomic group derived no measurable benefit from digital health interventions for physical activity (Western, 2021). Understanding why requires looking beyond infrastructure and towards the psychological, social, and motivational factors that shape whether someone can truly benefit from digital health.

Existing Frameworks and Their Limits

Several theoretical traditions have contributed to our understanding of digital health engagement. Cognitive and motivational theories — including Social Cognitive Theory (Bandura, 1991) and Self-Determination Theory (Ryan, 2000) — offer accounts of how self-efficacy, motivation, and behavioural regulation shape health behaviour. Technology adoption models such as the Technology Acceptance Model (Davis, 1989) and the Unified Theory of Acceptance and Use of Technology (Venkatesh, 2003) explain initial adoption decisions based on perceived usefulness and ease of use. Digital health literacy frameworks such as eHEALS (Norman, 2006) capture individuals’ ability to find, evaluate, and apply digital health information. Structural and sociocultural frameworks — including Diffusion of Innovation Theory (Rogers, 2014), Domestication Theory (Silverstone 1993), and ecological models such as the Digital Health Rainbow (Jahnel, 2022) — situate digital health use within broader social and material contexts.

No single framework, however, integrates these domains into a coherent, measurable account of why some people benefit from digital health and others do not.

Introducing Digital Health Enablement

Digital Health Enablement (DHE) is a new psychological construct developed to address this gap, defined as:

An individual’s readiness and capability to use, engage with, and benefit from digital technologies for the purpose of maintaining health, preventing ill health, or in the management of health conditions.

We use the term enablement deliberately — to signal that variation in who benefits from digital health reflects not individual deficit, but differential access to the conditions that make capability real (Zheng, 2008). DHE is conceived as dimensional (not an all-or-nothing state), dynamic (varying with time, context, and circumstance), and relational (someone may be well-enabled to use a laptop but not a smartwatch).

DHE is structured around a progression through three levels: use, engagement, and benefit. Seven psychosocial sub-constructs shape an individual’s capacity to progress through this pathway, each modifiable through targeted support or intervention design. These proximal factors are shaped in turn by more distal influences — economic circumstances, cultural environment, and personal characteristics such as values, personality, and physical health. By assessing DHE, it becomes possible to identify where support is needed and to tailor digital health interventions accordingly.
